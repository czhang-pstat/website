Almost everything I have been focusing on neural networks
lives on the mathematical side, mostly the (lack of) learning theory.
The hardware was a black box that turned `loss.backward()` into numbers.
It was not for lack of interest.  I think I just let the phrase
"parallel computing" do all the work, as if multiplying matrices on ten
thousand cores were something that happens naturally rather than
something people had to build.

Recently that changed.  The more I read about how these models are
*actually* trained at scale, the more the engineering looks like its own
kind of marvel, and nothing captures it better than **mixed-precision
training (mpt)** [@micikevicius2018mixed].  The premise is almost impudently
romantic.  Do the arithmetic of a billion-parameter model at half the usual
precision, get it roughly twice as fast on half the memory, and somehow
still converge to the same place.  The idea earned dedicated silicon in
2017, the *Tensor Cores* that now ship with every NVIDIA GPU, and has since
pushed past FP16 to FP8, with FP4 on the research frontier.  Naturally, I
had to write some small CUDA kernels and watch it work :)

The experiment is one operation computed four ways.  Matrix multiplications
in the form of $C = AB$ are the atoms of a network's forward and backward pass,
so we will race these building blocks.

- **Path A** runs on Tensor Cores with FP16 inputs and **FP16** accumulation, the naive half-precision version.
- **Path B** runs on Tensor Cores with FP16 inputs and **FP32** accumulation, mixed precision as recommended.
- **Path C** runs on ordinary CUDA cores in FP32 throughout, the full-precision baseline.
- **Path D** calls cuBLAS with FP16 inputs and FP32 accumulation, NVIDIA's hand-tuned kernel.

Everything is checked against an FP64 reference on the host.  I want to
answer two questions.  How much speed does the dedicated hardware buy, and
what does it cost?

#### My machine

All of this runs on my laptop, an ASUS ROG Zephyrus G16 (GA605, model year 2024),
which is worth introducing because two of its numbers will matter later.  The GPU
is an RTX 4070 Laptop GPU on the Ada architecture,
with 36 streaming multiprocessors and 8 GB of GDDR6.  Being the mobile
variant, its total graphics power is capped at 105 W, an 85 W base plus
20 W of Dynamic Boost, roughly half of the 200 W its desktop big brother
enjoys.  That cap turns out to shape the results as much as the silicon
does.  The host is an AMD Ryzen AI 9 HX 370 with 12 Zen 5 cores and 32 GB
of RAM.  AMD rates the chip at a 28 W default TDP, though this chassis
sustains it at roughly 65 to 80 W under full load.  The software stack
is Ubuntu 26.04 LTS, CUDA toolkit 13.3, and NVIDIA driver 610.43.

A brief word on measurement.  Each kernel is timed with CUDA events,
timestamps the GPU records in its own command stream, so the numbers
reflect actual GPU execution rather than host-side noise; every figure is
the average of ten launches after three untimed warmups.  Clock speed,
power draw, and temperature are sampled every 100 ms with `nvidia-smi`,
which reads the card's on-board telemetry, running in the background while
the benchmarks run.

#### Throughput is what we are watching

To keep the comparison fair I fix the shape to square matrices with
$M = N = K$ and vary only the scale.  Here are all four paths, in TFLOP/s.

| $M = N = K$ | A: FP16 acc | B: FP32 acc | C: FP32 cores | D: cuBLAS |
| ----------: | ----------: | ----------: | ------------: | --------: |
| $512$       | 5.6         | 5.7         | 1.1           | 16.6      |
| $1024$      | 6.0         | 6.0         | 1.2           | 21.4      |
| $2048$      | 6.8         | 6.8         | 1.3           | 39.3      |
| $4096$      | **7.1**     | **7.6**     | 1.1           | **42.4**  |
| $8192$      | 5.6         | 4.3         | 1.1           | 26.3      |

The table reads best one column at a time, because each path is fighting
a different limit.  Once each column makes sense on its own, the
comparisons across them fall out almost for free.

**Path C is flat because it fights only itself.**  The FP32 baseline posts
1.1 TFLOP/s at every size.  It is a same-effort naive kernel, its
bottleneck is its own memory traffic, and that bottleneck does not care
how big the problem is.  Its job in the table is to set the floor.

**Paths A and B are pinned to the memory wall.**  A GPU has two speeds,
how fast it computes and how fast it reads memory, and on this card they
are about 95 TFLOP/s against 256 GB/s.  Keeping the Tensor Cores fed
therefore requires roughly 370 FLOPs of work per byte fetched.  My naive
kernel offers 8 FLOPs/byte.  Each warp fetches two fresh $16\times16$ FP16 tiles
(1024 bytes) from global memory for every multiply & accumulate step
(8192 FLOPs) with no cache or reuse of the fetched data.  The computing units
spend most of their time waiting.  More importantly, simple calculation can verify
that the 8 FLOPs/bype of this naive kernel does not
grow with the size of matrix dimensions MNK, it is a scheduling problem not a size
problem.  That one fact explains the whole trend of both columns, that is,
the level is low and the climb from 5.6 TFLOP/s to 7.6 TFLOP/s is mild.  The memory
ceiling caused by scheduling does not grow with problem size.  In addition,
one can observe that the two columns tie at every size, even though on consumer Ada GPUs
FP16 accumulation has *twice* the theoretical compute throughput of FP32
accumulation.  That 2x is a gap between two ceilings, roughly 190 against
95 TFLOP/s, and my kernel idles at 7 while its Tensor Cores wait on
memory.  Doubling a ceiling you never touch changes nothing, so the 2x
simply disappears.

**Path D is the only column that shows the machine itself.**  cuBLAS is
tuned hard enough that its limits are the hardware's, which is why its
column is the only one with real dynamic range.  At $512^3$ it posts
16.6 TFLOP/s not because it is slow but because it is underemployed.  A
$512\times512$ output cuts into too few independent tiles of work for
36 SMs (in my kernel's terms, $32\times32 = 1024$ warp tiles, less than
one full wave for the machine), and the whole multiplication is over in about
50 microseconds, before the GPU even ramps its clocks up from idle.  As
the problem grows, the machine fills, the clock boosts to about 2565 MHz,
the card pulls its full 105 W, which delivers an formidable **42.4 TFLOP/s**
(considering a power capped mobile card).

**The last row hurts everyone.**  At $8192^3$ every path regresses; cuBLAS
drops from 42.4 to 26.3 and my path B from 7.6 to 4.3.  Each $8192^2$ FP32
matrix is 268 MB, so the working tiles no longer fit in cache, traffic
spills to DRAM, and the sustained full-power run throttles the clock a bit
further.  The knee at $4096^3$ is where the problem is big enough to
saturate the SMs but still small enough to live in cache.  Note that Path A's clear
win over Path B in this row, 5.6 vs 4.3, comes from its FP16 output being half the
bytes.  It is a memory saving, not a compute win.

Now put the columns side by side at the sweet spot of $4096^3$.  Even my
clumsy one-tile-per-warp kernel (Path A and B) beats the same-effort Path C FP32 baseline
**7x**.  That is the marvel in one line.  A chunk of the die is given over
to small FP16 multiplies, and it repays its transistors many times over.
cuBLAS beats the baseline roughly **38x**, and its 6x lead over my kernel is pure
software.  Shared-memory tiling, register blocking, and scheduling let it
do hundreds of FLOPs with each fetched byte before letting it go, hoisting
it off the memory wall that pins me.  Same silicon, same instruction.
"Using the hardware" and "using the hardware well" are different
achievements.  And FP16 accumulation buys essentially no speed anywhere in
the table.  Which sharpens the next question.  If it is not even faster,
what does it cost?

#### The price is precision

A matrix multiply is built from dot products of length $K$, the shared
inner dimension of $A_{M\times K}$ and $B_{K\times N}$, so $K$ counts how
many terms we accumulate.  Fix $M = N = 512$, sweep $K$, and watch the
relative error $\|\hat C - C\|_F / \|C\|_F$ against the FP64 reference.

|   $K$ | A: FP16 acc | B: FP32 acc | C: FP32 cores | D: cuBLAS |
| ----: | ----------- | ----------- | ------------- | --------- |
|   256 | 8.9e-04     | 2.9e-04     | 2.9e-07       | 2.9e-04   |
|   512 | 1.2e-03     | 2.9e-04     | 4.1e-07       | 2.9e-04   |
|  1024 | 1.7e-03     | 3.0e-04     | 5.7e-07       | 3.0e-04   |
|  2048 | 2.4e-03     | 2.9e-04     | 8.1e-07       | 2.9e-04   |
|  4096 | 3.3e-03     | 2.9e-04     | 1.1e-06       | 2.9e-04   |
|  8192 | **4.7e-03** | **2.9e-04** | 1.6e-06       | 2.9e-04   |

Path B sits flat at $2.9\times10^{-4}$ no matter what.  Path A starts worse
and *keeps worsening*, reaching a 16x gap by $K = 8192$.  Path C is three
orders of magnitude below both.  Path D matches path B to every digit the
program prints, a clue we will come back to.  One idea from floating-point
arithmetic explains the rest.

A float's mantissa sets its *relative* resolution.  FP16 carries 11
significant bits (10 stored plus one implicit), so near a number of
magnitude $s$ the representable values are spaced about $2^{-10}|s|$
apart, a gap known as the machine epsilon.  Rounding to the nearest
representable value therefore misses by at most half that gap, and this
half, the *unit roundoff*
$$
u_{16} = 2^{-11} \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-4},
$$
is the relative error a single FP16 operation can inject.  FP32's
counterpart is $u_{32} = 2^{-24} \approx 6.0 \times 10^{-8}$.  A dot
product is a running sum, $s_k = s_{k-1} + a_k b_k$, and every addition
rounds to the nearest representable number, injecting relative error up to
$u$.  The products carry random signs, so the errors random-walk and grow
like
$$
\text{error} \;\sim\; \sqrt{K}\,u .
$$
The failure mode even has a name, *swamping*.  Once the partial sum $s$ is
large and the next term is small enough that $|a_k b_k| < u\,|s|$, half
the local spacing, the addition rounds straight back to $s$ and the term
vanishes without a trace.  A long FP16 sum quietly stops accumulating.

That model explains the whole table.

- **B is flat because its error is floored by the inputs, not the sum.**
  Rounding the entries of $A$ and $B$ to FP16 injects a fixed error of
  order $u_{16}$.  With the accumulation kept in FP32, that floor
  of $2.9\times10^{-4}$ is the entire error, at $K = 256$ or $8192$ alike.
- **A grows because it stacks $\sqrt K\,u_{16}$ on top of the
  floor.**  Check the exponent.  $K$ grows 32x across the table,
  $\sqrt{32}\approx 5.7$, and the measured error grows 5.3x, tracking the
  random walk almost row for row.
- **C is the same story with $u_{32}$ up front**, an error near
  $10^{-6}$ that barely stirs.

So "accumulate in FP32" is not numerical fussiness.  It is the difference
between an error *bounded by your inputs* and one that *degrades with the
depth of your linear algebra*.  And in a real model $K$ is a layer width or
a sequence length, routinely in the thousands.

#### One template parameter

The accumulator precision is a native mode of the Tensor Core, not a
software workaround.  That is the point of this section, and it is why
paths A and B are the same kernel differing in a single template
parameter.

```cpp
template <typename AccT>
__global__ void wmmaGemm(const half *A, const half *B, AccT *C, int M, int N, int K) {
  wmma::fragment<wmma::matrix_a, 16, 16, 16, half, wmma::row_major> aFrag;
  wmma::fragment<wmma::matrix_b, 16, 16, 16, half, wmma::row_major> bFrag;
  wmma::fragment<wmma::accumulator, 16, 16, 16, AccT> acc;   // <- the only difference
  wmma::fill_fragment(acc, static_cast<AccT>(0.0f));
  // ...
  for (int k = 0; k < K; k += 16) {
    wmma::load_matrix_sync(aFrag, /* tile of A */);
    wmma::load_matrix_sync(bFrag, /* tile of B */);
    wmma::mma_sync(acc, aFrag, bFrag, acc);   // acc = aFrag * bFrag + acc, summed in AccT
  }
  // ...
}
```

The Tensor Core primitive works one level below our problem $C = AB$.
Writing lowercase for $16\times16$ tiles, a single warp-level instruction
computes the fused multiply-accumulate
$$
d \;=\; a\,b + c ,
$$
where $a$ and $b$ are FP16 tiles and $c$ is the running sum, which is how
the loop above folds the whole shared dimension into one accumulator.  The
instruction fixes the input type at FP16 but lets the accumulator be FP16
or FP32, and since the accumulator lives in registers, the choice changes
nothing about the bytes fetched or the multiplies performed.  Path A and
path B therefore do identical work on identical data and differ only in
where the sum is kept, which is precisely the comparison the two error
columns measured.  It also explains a detail in the data.  cuBLAS and my
path B produce bit-identical error columns, because both are running the
same instruction in the same mode, and all of NVIDIA's tuning shows up in
the throughput table instead.

#### But why is any of this allowed?

Step back and notice something uncomfortable.  Even path B, the *good* one,
is wrong by $2.9\times10^{-4}$.  We round every input to three or four
significant figures before multiplying, on the central operation of a model
with billions of parameters, millions of times per training step.  Yet the
trained model reaches the same validation accuracy as its FP32-trained
twin, with matching learning curves along the way; that is the central
experimental result of the mixed-precision paper.  In most of numerical
computing, throwing away four digits of your inputs to go faster would be
malpractice; you would never solve an ill-conditioned linear system that
way.

So why is a neural network so forgiving of noise that would wreck a careful
numerical algorithm, noise we not only tolerate but engineer whole chips to
inject?  That question lands squarely back on the
learning-theory side of things.  Something about how these models are
trained, and about the geometry of the functions they represent, makes them
robust to exactly this kind of perturbation.  That is the story for next
time.
