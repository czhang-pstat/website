This post collects some thoughts from my recent reading on what tensor
products really are.

A friend recently gifted me a book on *signature* methods and their
applications in machine learning and finance [@bayer2025signature]; it
is an excellent text to which he himself contributed.  A colleague and I were
immediately pulled in by the gravity of the Stone–Weierstrass theorem in this
setting, which, loosely speaking, says that any continuous function of a path
can be approximated, to any desired accuracy, by a linear functional of the
path's signature (finally, linear regression could be correct!).  That was enough to
get us to start a working group around the topic.

As we worked through the chapters, tensor products became a
ubiquitous theme.  My own exposure to them had never gone past
superficially identifying them with multidimensional arrays.  For
practical purposes that picture had served me well enough, but it always left
a hole that I understood neither the why nor the how.

After several reading and discussion sessions, and with the help of an
excellent note [@conrad_tensorprod], a couple of insights surfaced.
Before getting to the *why*, though, let's start with a concrete
view: how to construct a tensor product space.

Consider two vector spaces $U, V$ over a field $F$.  We build $U \otimes V$ in
three steps.

#### Step 1 — A free vector space

Construct a free vector space $\mathcal{F}(U \times V)$ which contains elements
in the form of
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n c_i (u_i, v_i),
$$
where $c_i \in F$, $u_i \in U$, and $v_i \in V$.

At this stage $\mathcal{F}(U \times V)$ is nothing more than the collection of
finite sums of such pairs, scaled by scalars from $F$; we have imposed no
structure on it yet.  In particular, we say nothing about how
$c_1 (u_1 + u_2, v_1)$ relates to $(c_1 u_1, v_1) + (c_1 u_2, v_1)$, even if,
having peeked ahead at what tensor products are, you are tempted to call them
equal.

#### Step 2 — The relations

Construct the subspace $W$ spanned by all elements of the following forms
(for all $u_1, u_2 \in U$, $v_1, v_2 \in V$, and $c \in F$):

1. $(u_1 + u_2, v_1) - (u_1, v_1) - (u_2, v_1)$
2. $(u_1, v_1 + v_2) - (u_1, v_1) - (u_1, v_2)$
3. $c(u_1, v_1) - (c u_1, v_1)$
4. $c(u_1, v_1) - (u_1, cv_1)$

#### Step 3 — The quotient

Take
$$
U \otimes V \vcentcolon = \mathcal{F}(U \times V) / W.
$$

In other words, elements in $U \otimes V$ are equivalence classes (cosets) of
elements of $\mathcal{F}(U \times V)$.  The cosets $(u, v) + W$ are called
the *pure* (or *simple*) tensors, which we denote
$u \otimes v$.  A general element, however, need not be a single pure tensor.
It is a finite sum
$$
\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_i (u_i, v_i)\Big) + W = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \, (u_i \otimes v_i),
$$
and in general this sum does not necessarily collapse to one $u \otimes v$.

Most texts on tensor products list the following properties as the most
descriptive characteristics.  One can verify that they hold for $U \otimes V$:
1. $(u_1 + u_2) \otimes v = u_1 \otimes v + u_2 \otimes v $, and
2. $u \otimes (v_1 + v_2) = u \otimes v_1 + u \otimes v_2 $, and
3. $c (u \otimes v) =  (cu) \otimes v = u \otimes (cv) $,

where $u_1, u_2, u \in U$, $v_1, v_2, v \in V$, and $c \in F.$

For example, let's check property 1.  By definition one has
$$
\begin{align*}
& (u_1 + u_2) \otimes v \\
= & (u_1 + u_2, v) + W \\
= & (u_1 + u_2, v) - [(u_1 + u_2, v) - (u_1, v) - (u_2, v)] + W \\
= & (u_1, v) + (u_2, v) + W \\
= & u_1 \otimes v + u_2 \otimes v.
\end{align*}
$$

This is no accident: by passing to the quotient $\mathcal{F}(U \times V) / W$
we have declared every element of $W$ to be the zero element.

I love this explicit construction because it lays the structure of
$U \otimes V$ completely bare.  In comparison, the isomorphism $\mathbb{R}^2
\otimes \mathbb{R}^2 \cong M_{2\times2}$ with the 2-by-2 matrices loses no
information; what the matrix shorthand *hides* is that many
different pairs $(u, v)$ collapse to the same tensor which is exactly what
the equivalence-class structure that the quotient makes explicit.
For instance, the following elements all represent the same member
in $\mathbb{R}^2 \otimes \mathbb{R}^2$:

$$
\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
3\\
\end{bmatrix}
\right),
6\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}
\right),
2\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}
\right),
3\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
2\\
\end{bmatrix}
\right).
$$

The more important question is *why* we go to all this trouble.  In short,
$U \otimes V$ together with the map $T: U \times V \rightarrow U \otimes V$
is the most general construction through which
every bilinear map factors: for any bilinear $B: U \times V \rightarrow P$
there is a unique linear map $L: U \otimes V \rightarrow P$ with
$B = L \circ T.$

My train arrived.  Let's leave the even more interesting discussion on the
*why* part to the next post.
