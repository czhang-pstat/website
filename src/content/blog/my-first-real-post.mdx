This post is about some thinking after relearning what tensor products are.

Recently a friend gifted me a book on signature methods in finance, which
is an awesome book to which he also made a great contribution.  A colleague
and I immediately got pulled by the gravity of the the Stone-Weierstrass
theorem in this context, that any continuous function of a path can be
approximated to any desired degree of accuracy by a linear functional
of the path signatures (finally linear regression could be correct!). So
we started a working group to expore more on this topic.

As we dive into the chapters, tensor products become a ubiquitous theme.
My previous exposure to tensor products are limited to the superfical
level of identifying them with multidimensional arrays.  For all practical
purposes it seemed to have serve me sufficiently well despite the
unsatisfactory feeling for the lack of understanding of why and how.

After several reading and discussion sessions and with the help of this
excellent note, here are a couple of new thoughts.

Before diving into the why part, Let's first look at a concrete example
on how to construct a sufficiently general tensor product space.

Consider two vector spaces $U, V$ over a field $F$.

* Step 1
Construct the free vector space $\mathcal{F}(U \times V)$ which contains elements
in the form of
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n c_i (u_i \times v_i),
$$
where $c_i \in F$, $u_i \in U$, and $v_i \in V$.

Note that at this stage $\mathcal{F}(U \times V)$ is simply a collection of finite sums
of pairs from $U \times V$ scalled by scalars from the underlying field $F$.  We have
not defined any structure over it.  For instance, we are not saying anything about
the relationship between $c_1 (u_1 + u_2, v_1)$ and $c_1 (u_1, v_1) + c_1 (u_2, v_1)$,
even though you might be tempted to say that they equal to each other if you have peaked
into what tensor products are.

* Step 2
Construct a subspace $W$ which contains elements in the following forms:

1. $(u_1 + u_2, v_1) - (u_1, v_1) - (u_2, v_1)$
2. $(u_1, v_1 + v_2) - (u_1, v_1) - (u_1, v_2)$
3. $c_1(u1, v_1) - (c_1 u_1, v_1)$
4. $c_1(u1, v_1) - (u_1, c_1v_1)$

* Step 3
Take
$$
U \otimes V \vcentcolon = \mathcal{F}(U \times V) / W.
$$

In other words, elements in $U \otimes V$ are equivalence classes in the form of cosets
$(u, v) + W$ for $u \in U, v \in V.$
